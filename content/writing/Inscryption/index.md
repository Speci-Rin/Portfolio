### Abstract
Game designers have different ways in approaching creating games. This paper examines these methods through the characters in the game Inscryption labeled as “Scrybes.” These characters were used as a basis on which to build a game designer taxonomy, similar to how there have been player type taxonomies. This new way of categorizing game designers will split the different types into four categories, mirroring the four characters in Inscryption. Then, this taxonomy will be compared to player type taxonomies, such as Barthle’s Taxonomy of Player Types and the taxonomy in Ron Edwards’s System Does Matter. Through organizing different game designers in these categories, we will be able to better understand their games and characteristics within these games.
## Inscryption: The Hidden Taxonomy of Game Designer Types
Throughout the game of Inscryption, you meet the four different Scrybes, each one having their own unique characterization. These main themes of the game amount to a commentary on different types of game designers. These characterizations may be used to create a taxonomy of game designers that is similar to existing taxonomies of player types. Being a certain “player type” is language that is often used when describing how a certain person prefers to play a game, but it also implies there are “designer types” that describes how certain designers prefer to make their games. By categorizing game designers in this way, we can avoid linking them to specific game attributes – such as the “first person shooter designer” or the “puzzle game designer” – and better understand the reasons behind their work.&emsp;
There are currently a few widely accepted taxonomies of player types. One of these being Barthle’s Taxonomy of Player Types, which splits players up into four main categories: the Achiever, the Killer, the Explorer, and the Socializer. The Achiever searches to get achievements as fast and efficiently as possible. The Killer is similar in the desire to gain points but differs in the sense that they are highly competitive and want to see others lose. The Explorer is set on finding easter eggs or more hidden elements of a game. Lastly, the Socializer is focused on collaboration and interacting with other players (Kumar et al., 2017). Another taxonomy is that from System Does Matter where Ron Edwards (2004) expands on player types commonly found in RPG settings. In this, he describes three player outlooks. A Gamist player describes those who are focused on competition and optimization. Simulationist players focus more on the creation of systems to create more organic game play. Lastly, Narrativist players have a story-first approach to games that is most often found in role playing games. Generally, these characterizations are made to help designers cater to different players – to build a world in which a certain player would gain the most enjoyment. These taxonomies are to make categories in order to better understand what kinds of audiences there are. If game designers are using categories like these to cater their games to people, this begs for a complementary taxonomy of game designers themselves. &emsp;
Inscryption is the perfect medium from which to build this taxonomy due to the four Scrybes. As revealed in Act 2, each Scrybe is fighting for control over the game, and they morph the game into their perfect version of it when they do gain control. We see this fully in Act 1 and Act 3 when Leshy and P03 take over the game respectively, and a glimpse of this is seen with the other Scrybes during the final moments when the game is being deleted. Each Scrybe has a different approach to the base game of Inscryption that the player character, Luke, finds in the woods. They act as the “Game Master” for the player. These varied strategies represent the individualized approaches to game design that each designer embodies.&emsp;
Starting with the first Scrybe we meet, Leshy takes an immediate narrative lead. He narrates the player as they travel through the woods of his story and even acts out different characters by putting on masks. It is also notable that ludic elements of the game are simpler compared to the other acts later on. Actions such as making a card when you die are also more restricted. In Leshy’s domain, characteristics can be taken from cards already in your deck to create a new card instead of selecting multiple different abilities and strengths from a list as is done in P03’s domain. Additionally, while the name is the only part of the card where creative control exists, it is also the aspect involving no mechanics. This reminds me of Espen Aareth’s writing, A Narrative Theory of Games, when he states, “a game which allows great player freedom in creating or modifying objects will at the same time not be able to afford strong narrative control” (Aarseth, 2012). The restrictions in creating cards and lack of ludic complexity allows room for the narrative to be the focus of the game. &emsp;
All of this equates to Leshy representing the story-first designers. This type of game designer leads with the story, and it is the most important part of the game. For the sake of taxonomy, I will call them the “Story Tellers”. A Story Teller’s game may not have complicated gameplay, and the point of their game is not for the player character to focus on leveling up. In fact, the players’ choices may have no effect on the ending to prioritize the message. which gives the illusion of choice. This designer is creating a narrative for the player to experience through their game, and the leading hook to these games is the story and themes. Players that might enjoy the Story Teller’s work, using terms from the player taxonomies previously mentioned, would be the Narrativist gamers.&emsp;
P03 is the Scrybe that takes over the game in Act 3, completely changing specific parts of the game, yet still keeping the core of it. He takes an optimization first approach to his domain. In contrast to Leshy’s limited ludic elements, P03 immediately adds many mechanics and features to the game. For instance, he creates a fifth battle spot on the board for no other reason than to add a layer of complexity. He also adds the game play of each of the three other Scrybe’s decks that were introduced in Act 2. All of this provides freedom for the player to choose different play styles. Speaking of cards, P03 allows the player to make cards from a selection of up to 4 abilities as well as choosing the initial cost, power, and health. This forces the player to optimize a card to fit their deck and gives them an advantage. Going by Aareth’s quote from earlier, allowing the player more freedom in the creation of game objects gives the creator much less narrative freedom, but allows for more complex gameplay. The story of P03’s domain is extremely simple, and the player is mostly wandering to find battles, upgrades, or money. The direction is a lot less structured than what we previously saw in Leshy’s story where there were different pathways, but it ultimately led you to the same conclusion. &emsp;
Therefore, P03 represents the game designers who are focused on the ludic parts of the game. The term “Optimizer” might fit this characterization well. This type of game designer, like P03, is focused on the mechanics of the game and what strategies they may be able to force the player to figure out. These games may lack a lot of narrative – perhaps even to the point of creating complex systems that interact instead of any specific story. The Optimizer would make games that appeal to the Gamist and Simulationist players who would enjoy the competitive side to figuring out the most optimized player character or the systems resulting from a highly mechanical game respectively. These games would also appeal to the achiever and killer players outlined in Bartle’s Taxonomy. Both of these players are focused on the ludic elements of a game and how to either gain the achievement or ultimately win the game.&emsp;
The next two Scrybes are seen less by the player, yet they are still able to convey two other game designer types despite their lack of screen time. Starting with Magnificus, his entire character is centered around art and creation. In Act 2, we see him in his tower, painting on a canvas. In fact, the way in which he creates cards, as described in the same act, is to paint his students. Magnificus also seems to be heavily tied with the act of creating things. This is not only shown through his theme of painting but also in the last act, he is the Scrybe to offer the player the “new game” button. After this, during the final moments when the game is about to be deleted, we see a glimpse into what his domain might have looked like. By placing the cards on the table, a physical being is created on the larger player board in front of them. This theme of creation aligns with his art, but also alludes to creating for the sake of art. &emsp;
This designer type can be labeled the “Visionary”. The Visionary makes aesthetic-forward games, and their themes often align with the creation of art with games as a medium. The games this designer makes may be more visually appealing with a focus on the “feel” of a game rather than the specific mechanics or narrative. Games that the Visionary may make could be in the cozy game genre since they often have little story and simple mechanics. I feel none of the player taxonomies I had previously mentioned particularly fit under these games, so perhaps another player type could be defined for players looking for games made by this particular game designer.&emsp;
Lastly, Grimora, as a character, is more difficult to pin when thinking about her in the context of game designers. Throughout the game, she is depicted as very friendly and social. She even has the best relationship with each of the other Scrybes despite the conflict. When she was the Stinkbug and P03 was the Stoat, P03 treated her more amicably than any other character, including – or perhaps especially – the player character. Magnificus, who does not seem to care for anyone including his own students, also seems to view her in a more positive light since he sent Grimora a letter warning her about P03’s plot. She also treats the player character kindly throughout the whole game, complimenting moves and encouraging them. Grimora seems to enjoy the act of playing the game, and she is more than happy to spend time with the characters around her. During the last scenes of the game, we are finally able to see what Grimora’s domain might have looked like. Here, she mourns the fun you would have had playing the game with her, focusing on the interaction between herself and the player character. &emsp;
Grimora depicts the designers focused on socialization and connecting with other players. Let’s call these designers the “Match Makers”. The Match Makers create games so that players can make connections with one another and build friendships. They have succeeded when players are able to form different relationships when playing their games. The player type that would most enjoy the Match Maker’s game is, of course, the Socializer who seeks this connection.&emsp;
In the end, designers use elements from each area to create their games, but they often lead with a certain experience in mind. The designer types help explain this goal of specific designers when creating games. Although, much like the player types, it is ultimately a category that is used to describe how each designer may approach a game, not a box that a designer is forced to stay in. Understanding the different designer types can help players discover games they might be more interested in as well as other designers looking for a direction for their own game creations. By describing a designer as a Story Teller, Optimizer, Visionary, and Match Maker as seen through the characters of Inscryption, we are better able to understand their games and personal characteristics.
